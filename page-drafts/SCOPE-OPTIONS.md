# Scope options for the $250/month bookkeeping package

## The constraint that decides this

Your own rate is **$250 an hour**. Your monthly package is **$250 a month, flat, no overages**.

So the $250 tier is, by your own pricing, **one hour of work a month**. That is the boundary the
scope has to respect. Anything you include that pushes a typical client past an hour is
unprofitable at that price, and "no overages" means you cannot recover it.

One hour a month of bookkeeping realistically covers: reconciling one or two accounts with low
transaction volume, categorizing them, and producing two reports. It does not cover chasing
receipts, untangling personal spending, inventory, or a client who calls weekly.

**Everything below is a suggestion. Sanity-check each tier against how long that work actually
takes your team — you know that and I do not.**

---

## Option A — Three tiers, transaction-based (recommended)

The most common structure in this market, and the easiest for a buyer to self-select into.

| Tier | Price | Accounts | Transactions/month | Included |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Essentials | $250/mo | Up to 2 | Up to 100 | Monthly reconciliation, categorization, profit and loss, balance sheet |
| Standard | $500/mo | Up to 4 | Up to 300 | Everything above, plus payroll reports imported and a quarterly review call |
| Full | $850/mo | Up to 8 | Up to 750 | Everything above, plus inventory or class tracking and a monthly call |

Above the top tier, quoted individually. Anyone who outgrows a tier gets told before the change,
never after.

**Why this one.** The tiers land at roughly one, two and three and a half hours at your rate, so
each is priced against real delivery cost. It keeps "no overages" true within a tier, and the
re-quote happens at a tier boundary the client already agreed to, which is a conversation rather
than a surprise invoice.

**On the page:** show all three. A single price makes a buyer guess whether they qualify; three
tiers let them find themselves and make $250 read as the entry point it is.

---

## Option B — Two tiers, simplest to publish

| Tier | Price | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly bookkeeping | $250/mo | Up to 2 accounts and 100 transactions a month |
| Custom | Quoted | Anything larger, priced after we look at the accounts |

**Why this one.** Least work to write and least to maintain. It gives up the middle of the market,
where most of your revenue probably is, so I would only pick it if you want the page live this
week and will tier it properly later.

---

## Option C — Bound by accounts only, not transactions

| Tier | Price | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly bookkeeping | $250/mo | Up to 2 accounts, any volume |

**Why I would not.** Account count is easy for a buyer to understand and it is a poor proxy for
work. Two accounts running 800 transactions a month is many hours, and with no overages you
absorb all of it. Transaction volume is the honest variable, so it belongs in the boundary.

---

## What to write on the page, whichever you pick

Three sentences do the whole job:

> **$250 a month covers up to two accounts and 100 transactions a month** — reconciliation,
> categorization, and a profit and loss statement and balance sheet every month.
> **No hourly overages and no surprise invoices.**
> If your volume grows past the tier, we tell you before anything changes, never after.

That last sentence is the one competitors cannot write. Most of them bill the overage first and
explain it on the invoice.

---

## Two things to decide alongside the tier

**Who pays for the QuickBooks subscription?** Say so on the page. It is a real cost and buyers
comparing $250 against $199 elsewhere will ask.

**What happens when a client is behind at signup?** Almost every inbound bookkeeping client has
some catch-up to do first. That is cleanup, billed hourly at $250, and it should be stated as a
separate step on the page rather than discovered on the first call. It also routes them to page 1,
which is your best page.
