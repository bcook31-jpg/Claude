## Quick answer (first 40 to 50 words — write straight, no personality)

Most individual tax returns cost between $200 and $500 to have prepared. A return with
self-employment income, rental property or multiple states usually runs $400 to $900. Anyone
quoting a price before seeing your documents is guessing, and anyone charging a percentage of
your refund should be avoided.

## Body

### Why nobody will give you a number over the phone

You want a price. That is reasonable. The reason you keep getting a range is that the two
returns that take us forty minutes and four hours look identical when described out loud.

What actually drives the fee is how many forms your situation requires and how organized your
records are. A W-2 and a standard deduction is one form. Add a rental, a side business, some
stock sales and a second state, and you have added four more forms, each with its own schedule.

### What moves the price up

- **Self-employment income.** A Schedule C is the single biggest jump for most people.
- **Rental property.** One Schedule E per property.
- **Investment sales.** Especially if the basis is missing and has to be reconstructed.
- **Multiple states.** Each state return is priced separately.
- **A business entity.** An LLC, partnership or S corporation return is a separate filing from
  your personal one.
- **Disorganized records.** The National Society of Accountants found that 75.1% of preparers
  charge more for disorganized or incomplete paperwork, by an average of $145.
  [https://connect.nsacct.org/blogs/nsa-blogger/2017/01/27/national-society-of-accountants-reports-on-average-tax-return-preparation-fees]

That last one is the only item on the list you control, and it is worth real money. Bring your
documents sorted and you are not paying someone to sort them.

### The numbers people quote, and why to treat them carefully

Almost every article on tax preparation pricing traces back to the National Society of
Accountants fee survey. It is a real survey and it is worth knowing about, with one caveat
almost nobody mentions: **the 2020-2021 edition was the last one they produced.**

That means the figures circulating as current are, at best, adjusted from data several years
old. Adjusted into February 2025 dollars, the survey put a Form 1040 with a standard deduction
at about $273. An earlier edition put an itemized 1040 with a Schedule C and a state return at
about $457.

Use those as a sense of scale, not as a quote. Prices vary by region, and Southern California is
not the national average.

### Three ways preparers charge, and one to walk away from

**A flat fee per return type.** The most common and the easiest to compare. You know the price
before work starts.

**Per form.** Transparent, and it explains why the fee rose when your life got more complicated.

**Hourly.** Usually $100 to $400 an hour, more common for business and complex work.

**A percentage of your refund.** This is the one to walk away from. It gives the preparer a
direct financial interest in the size of your refund, which is precisely the wrong incentive to
introduce into a tax return.

### How to tell whether a quote is fair

1. **Ask what it includes.** Both federal and state? E-filing? A copy of the return? What happens
   if the IRS sends a letter?
2. **Ask what would make it go up.** A preparer who can answer specifically has actually looked
   at your situation.
3. **Ask who signs it.** Every paid preparer must sign the return and enter their PTIN. A
   preparer who will not sign is a serious problem, not a discount.
4. **Check the credential.** More on that below.

### Does the credential change the price?

Yes, and it is one of the more misunderstood parts of this. A CPA, an enrolled agent and a
registered tax preparer are three different things with three different price points, and the
most expensive is not automatically the right one for your return.

**[H2 SPOKE SECTION → page 18]** *How much a CPA costs, and when you actually need one.* A CPA
is a state-licensed accounting credential with unlimited rights to represent you before the IRS.
They generally cost the most. Read the full breakdown → [link page 18]

**[H2 SPOKE SECTION → page 19]** *What tax preparers charge.* Most returns in California are
prepared by CTEC-registered preparers and enrolled agents rather than CPAs, at a lower price
point. Read what they charge and what they are allowed to do → [link page 19]

### One California-specific thing worth knowing

California is one of only a few states that regulate paid tax preparers directly. Anyone
preparing returns for a fee in California must either hold an exempt credential — CPA, enrolled
agent, or California attorney — or be registered with the California Tax Education Council. CTEC
registration requires 60 hours of qualifying education, a $5,000 surety bond, a background check
and a PTIN, renewed annually.
[https://www.ftb.ca.gov/tax-pros/california-tax-education-council.html]

If someone is preparing your California return for money and is neither exempt nor CTEC
registered, they are operating unlawfully. It takes a minute to check.

## FAQ (straight, no personality — these get lifted verbatim by AI summaries)

**What is the average cost of tax preparation?**
Most individual returns fall between $200 and $500. Returns with self-employment, rental
property or multiple states typically run $400 to $900. The most-cited national survey, from the
National Society of Accountants, put a standard-deduction Form 1040 at roughly $273 in February
2025 dollars, though that survey was last produced for 2020-2021.

**Why is my tax preparation fee higher this year?**
Usually because your return got more complicated: a new income source, a property, a business, a
second state, or a change in filing status. Disorganized records also raise the fee.

**Is tax preparation cheaper online?**
For a simple return, yes — online filing typically runs $120 to $200. The gap closes quickly
once self-employment or rental income is involved, because those are the situations where a
preparer's judgment changes the outcome.

**Are tax preparation fees deductible?**
Not for W-2 employees taking the standard deduction. Fees related to preparing business
schedules are generally deductible as a business expense. Your situation decides it, so confirm
with your preparer.

**Should I pay a percentage of my refund?**
No.
