## Quick answer

A CPA typically charges $200 to $600 for an individual tax return and $500 to $2,000 or more for
a business return, or $150 to $400 an hour for advisory work. Most straightforward returns do not
require a CPA. An enrolled agent or a registered tax preparer can file the same return, usually
for less.

## Body

### What you are paying for

A CPA is a state-licensed credential. In California it requires a bachelor's degree with
specified accounting coursework, passing all four parts of the Uniform CPA Examination, a year of
qualifying experience, and ongoing continuing education. The licence is issued and policed by the
California Board of Accountancy.

That is a serious credential and the price reflects it. The question worth asking is whether your
return needs it.

### The comparison nobody puts in writing

Here is the part most articles on this keyword skip, because most of them are published by CPA
firms.

**Three types of professional can prepare your federal return for a fee**, and all three must
hold a PTIN and sign the return:

**Certified Public Accountant.** State-licensed. Unlimited rights to represent you before the
IRS. Broad accounting training beyond tax. Typically the most expensive.

**Enrolled Agent.** Federally licensed by the IRS, specifically in taxation. Also has **unlimited
rights to represent you before the IRS** on audits, collections and appeals — the same
representation rights as a CPA. This surprises most people. The credential is earned by passing a
three-part IRS examination or through qualifying IRS employment.
[https://www.irs.gov/tax-professionals/understanding-tax-return-preparer-credentials-and-qualifications]

**CTEC Registered Tax Preparer.** In California, anyone preparing returns for a fee who is not a
CPA, enrolled agent or attorney must register with the California Tax Education Council: 60 hours
of qualifying education, a $5,000 surety bond, a background check, a PTIN, and annual renewal
with 20 hours of continuing education.
[https://www.ftb.ca.gov/tax-pros/california-tax-education-council.html]

**Annual Filing Season Program participant.** A fourth tier most people have never heard of. It is
a voluntary IRS programme for preparers who are not CPAs, enrolled agents or attorneys. Completing
it earns a Record of Completion and **limited representation rights**: the preparer can represent
you before IRS revenue agents, customer service and the Taxpayer Advocate Service, but only on a
return they personally prepared and signed, and not before appeals or collections. Participants
are listed in the IRS public directory of preparers alongside CPAs and enrolled agents.
[https://www.irs.gov/tax-professionals/annual-filing-season-program]

The practical difference is representation, not preparation. All four can prepare and file the
same return. CPAs, enrolled agents and attorneys can represent you before any IRS office without
limitation. An Annual Filing Season Program participant can represent you in a narrower set of
situations. A registered preparer without that Record of Completion cannot represent you at all.

### So when do you actually need a CPA?

**Probably yes:**

- You need audited or reviewed financial statements — a bank or investor is asking for them, and
  only a CPA can issue them
- Complex multi-entity structures, or a business sale or acquisition
- Something is already in dispute with a taxing authority and you want one firm for the whole
  arc

**Probably not:**

- A W-2 return, even with a mortgage, children and investments
- A sole proprietorship or single-member LLC with clean books
- A straightforward S corporation return
- Rental property, unless the structure is unusual

### The honest version

Paying a CPA for a return an enrolled agent could file is not a mistake — it buys you a broader
bench if something unusual comes up. But it is money, and plenty of people spend it because they
assumed "CPA" was the only option rather than because they compared.

The reverse mistake is worse: choosing on price alone and ending up with someone unregistered
who will not sign the return.

### How to check anyone's credential in two minutes

- **CPA:** search the California Board of Accountancy licence lookup
- **Enrolled agent, CPA, or Annual Filing Season Program participant:** the IRS maintains a public
  directory that lists all of them
  [https://www.irs.gov/tax-professionals/faqs-directory-of-federal-tax-return-preparers-with-credentials-and-select-qualifications]
- **CTEC registered:** search the CTEC verification page [https://ctec.org/]

Do this before you hand anyone your documents. It takes less time than finding your W-2.

## FAQ

**Is a CPA worth it for a simple tax return?**
Usually not. A W-2 return with a standard deduction can be filed by any qualified preparer, and a
CPA's rate reflects training you are not using.

**Can an enrolled agent do everything a CPA can do for taxes?**
For tax preparation and IRS representation, effectively yes — enrolled agents have unlimited
representation rights before the IRS. CPAs can additionally issue audited and reviewed financial
statements, which enrolled agents cannot.

**Why is a CPA more expensive?**
A longer licensing path, broader scope of practice, and in many cases higher overhead.

**How much does a CPA charge per hour?**
Commonly $150 to $400, varying by region and specialty.

**Do I need a CPA for an LLC?**
No. An LLC return can be prepared by any qualified preparer. The entity type does not require a
particular credential.
