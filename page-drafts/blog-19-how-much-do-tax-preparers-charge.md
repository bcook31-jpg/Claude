## Quick answer

Most tax preparers charge $150 to $450 for an individual return and $400 to $1,200 for a small
business return, either as a flat fee per return type or per form. There is no legal cap on what
a preparer may charge, but charging a fee based on a percentage of your refund is prohibited for
most returns, and any preparer who refuses to sign your return is breaking federal law.

  why the fee changes year to year.
- **Hourly.** More common for business work and cleanup, generally $100 to $300.

### What a preparer may and may not charge — the actual rules

This is the part worth knowing, because it is where people get hurt.

**There is no legal ceiling on preparation fees.** Preparers set their own prices and the market
sorts it out. A high fee is not itself improper.

**Contingent fees are restricted.** Charging a fee based on a percentage of your refund is
prohibited for ordinary return preparation under Treasury Department Circular 230, which governs
practice before the IRS. Narrow exceptions exist for certain claims and audit situations, but for
the return you file every April, a percentage-of-refund arrangement is not permitted.

**Every paid preparer must sign your return** and include their Preparer Tax Identification
Number. This is a legal requirement, not a courtesy. A preparer who fills in your return and
leaves the paid-preparer section blank — a "ghost preparer" — is breaking the law, and you are
the one left holding the return.
[https://www.irs.gov/tax-professionals/ptin-requirements-for-tax-return-preparers]

**Your refund should go to you.** It should be deposited into your account, never into the
preparer's. Check the routing and account numbers on the return before you sign.

**In California, the preparer must be credentialed or registered.** CPAs, enrolled agents and
California attorneys are exempt. Everyone else preparing returns for a fee must be registered
with the California Tax Education Council, which requires 60 hours of qualifying education, a
$5,000 surety bond, a background check, a PTIN and annual renewal.
[https://www.ftb.ca.gov/tax-pros/california-tax-education-council.html]

That surety bond exists specifically to give you recourse. It is one of the few consumer
protections in this industry and most people never learn it is there.

### Red flags, in order of seriousness

1. **Will not sign the return.** Walk out. This is not negotiable.
2. **Fee is a percentage of your refund.** Prohibited for ordinary returns.
3. **Your refund routes to their account.** Never acceptable.
4. **Promises a refund amount before seeing your documents.** Nobody can know that.
5. **Not registered and not exempt.** Check it, in California it takes a minute.
6. **Asks you to sign a blank or incomplete return.** Never do this.

### What you should expect for your money

- Both the federal and the California return, e-filed
- A complete copy of what was filed
- The preparer's signature and PTIN on it
- A clear answer on what happens if a notice arrives later, and whether that costs extra

That last one catches people out. Ask before you engage, not after the letter arrives.

### Getting the fee down, legitimately

Bring your records organized. The National Society of Accountants found 75.1% of preparers charge
more for disorganized paperwork, by an average of $145.
[https://connect.nsacct.org/blogs/nsa-blogger/2017/01/27/national-society-of-accountants-reports-on-average-tax-return-preparation-fees]

Sorted documents, last year's return, and a complete list of income sources is genuinely the
difference between the bottom and the top of a quoted range.

## FAQ

**How much can a tax preparer legally charge?**
There is no legal maximum on a preparation fee. However, charging a fee based on a percentage of
your refund is prohibited for ordinary return preparation under Circular 230, and every paid
preparer must sign the return and provide a PTIN.

**Is it normal for a tax preparer to charge per form?**
Yes. Per-form pricing is common and it is one of the more transparent structures.

**What is a ghost preparer?**
Someone who prepares a return for a fee and does not sign it or enter a PTIN. It is unlawful, and
it leaves you solely responsible for a return you did not prepare.

**Do I have to pay before the return is filed?**
Practices vary. What matters is that your refund is never routed through the preparer's account.

**How do I check whether a California preparer is registered?**
Search the CTEC verification page at ctec.org, or the IRS directory for credentialed preparers.
