## Quick answer (first 40–50 words, straight)

Starting a California non-profit takes two separate approvals. First you incorporate with the
California Secretary of State. Then you apply to the IRS for 501(c)(3) tax-exempt status, which is
a separate application with its own fee. Two further California registrations follow, and most
guides omit both.

## Body

### The part most guides get wrong

Search this question and you will find a lot of articles that stop after the IRS application. Two
California requirements come after it, and skipping them is how a new non-profit ends up
suspended or unable to fundraise legally.

- **Franchise Tax Board exemption.** Federal 501(c)(3) status does not automatically exempt you
  from California franchise tax. That is a separate application.
- **Registry of Charities and Fundraisers.** Most charitable organisations must register with the
  California Attorney General, and renew annually.

⚠️ **[VERIFY both, including form numbers, fees and renewal timing, against the FTB and the
Attorney General directly. These are the two facts that make this post better than everything
ranking above it, so they have to be exactly right.
https://www.ftb.ca.gov/ · https://oag.ca.gov/charities]**

### The steps, in order

⚠️ [Verify every fee and timeline before publishing. Never print a fee sourced from a blog,
including this draft.]

1. **Decide whether a non-profit is actually the right structure.** Not every good cause needs
   one. If the plan is to pay yourself for services, or the funding is a handful of private
   donors, a different structure may serve better. Say so honestly — it is the sentence that makes
   a reader trust the rest.
2. **Choose and check the name** with the Secretary of State.
3. **Recruit the board.** California sets minimums for directors, and the IRS looks closely at
   whether the board is genuinely independent.
4. **File articles of incorporation** with the Secretary of State. [Verify current fee.]
5. **Get an EIN** from the IRS. Free, and takes minutes online.
6. **Adopt bylaws and a conflict-of-interest policy**, and hold the first board meeting.
7. **Apply to the IRS for exemption** — Form 1023, or the shorter 1023-EZ if you qualify.
   [Verify current user fees and eligibility thresholds.]
   https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/application-for-recognition-of-exemption
8. **Apply for California exemption** with the Franchise Tax Board.
9. **Register with the Attorney General's Registry.**
10. **File the initial Statement of Information** within 90 days of incorporating. [Link page 32.]

### The mistakes that cost the most later

- **Bylaws copied from a template that does not match how the board actually operates.** They
  govern real decisions and get read in a dispute.
- **A board that is not independent enough**, which the IRS scrutinises.
- **No bookkeeping from day one.** Restricted funds have to be tracked from the first donation.
  Reconstructing that later is expensive. [Link page 33.]
- **Missing the annual filings.** Exempt status is revoked automatically after three consecutive
  missed years.

### Doing it yourself

You can. The filings are public and the instructions are published, and a founder with time and
patience can complete all of them.

What people usually underestimate is not the incorporation, it is the exemption application and
the ongoing compliance that follows. If you want to do it yourself, the links above are where to
start and we would rather you succeed than sell you something you did not need.

**Do I need a lawyer?**
Not usually. Incorporation and the exemption application are filings a firm that does them
regularly can handle. A lawyer is worth it where the structure is unusual, or where property,
litigation or complex governance is involved.

**Can I pay myself from a non-profit?**
Yes, reasonable compensation for actual work is allowed and is common. What is not allowed is
private benefit, where the organisation's earnings flow to insiders. The distinction matters and
the IRS looks at it.

**What is the difference between a non-profit and a 501(c)(3)?**
Non-profit is the state corporate form. 501(c)(3) is federal tax-exempt status. You can be one
without the other, and you generally want both.

**Do we have to file a return if we have no money?**
Yes. Even organisations below the filing threshold must submit an annual electronic notice, and
three consecutive misses revokes exempt status automatically.
